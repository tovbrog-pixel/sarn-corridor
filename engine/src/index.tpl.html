How to command

  • Map: drag to pan, wheel to zoom, right-drag to rotate and tilt. Keys: WASD pan, Q/E rotate, R/F tilt, Z/X zoom. O, or Map on the top bar, puts the whole map in view. The view never drops to ground level.
  • Keys: Tab and Shift+Tab step through your formations and frame each with its objective. Enter issues the order you have drawn. Esc throws away a drawing, or clears the selection. The full stop (.) runs to the next event.
  • Forces list: formations are grouped by division; click a division's name to fold it. Compact list shows one line per formation. To staff on a division, or Hand to staff on a card, lets the staff run those formations: it follows each one's plan from here and picks its own objectives when the plan runs out. Issuing an order yourself takes a formation back.
  • Panels: drag the Forces panel by its :: grip, and the card, the reports and the chief of staff's panel by their title bars. Double-click a bar to put the panel back. The – buttons fold a panel down to its bar. UI on the top bar sets the size of the panels and text. All of this is remembered in this browser.
  • Orders: after you draw a line of advance, a dotted line shows the road route the colonel is likely to take. A red X and a warning mark a river with no open bridge, or a route so long the formation would halt. Add next leg on the card queues a second order that starts when the present objective is reached. The staff prepares it at once, so it costs no time later.
  • The clock: under the Forces list, choose which reports stop the clock: contact (slows to 1x), a bridge blown or broken, a formation cut off, an order refused, an objective changing hands, a crossing under fire. Next event runs at 40x and stops at the next report that needs you. When the clock stops or slows on its own, a red bar at the foot of the map says why; its button restarts the clock. One tick box chooses whether the camera moves to the place; another whether the clock stops when you switch to another window. The clock keeps the chosen speed when the frame rate drops.
  • Frontage: a battalion holds about 2 km of front in open ground and 1 km in woods, towns or broken country. When you draw a line longer than the forward battalions can hold, the card says so, and the formation holds a solid sector at the centre of the line. Stretch along the whole line spreads them across all of it, with gaps between battalions. Up close, a bar under each battalion in the line shows the sector it holds; enemy sectors show only where your troops have seen them in the last 30 minutes.
  • Rivers on a line: if the objective line you draw crosses a river with no bridge, the formation holds only the stretch on its own bank, nearest where its line of advance meets the line. The card says so.
  • Flanks: tanks and tank destroyers carry thinner armour on their sides. A shot from outside a unit's front arc (more than about 60 degrees off its facing) meets the side plate, which is how a 75mm Sherman kills a Panther. A battalion holding a line faces square to its own stretch of it, so a curved perimeter faces outward all round. One on the move faces the way it is going. The log tells you when one of your battalions catches an enemy in the flank.
  • Mixed guns: a few units carry two main guns and fire each at its own chance: British Sherman regiments with one 17-pounder Firefly per troop of four, US late-1944 tank battalions with some 76mm Shermans, British anti-tank batteries with 6- and 17-pounders.
  • Opening positions: formations start in solid sectors. Where history had them spread thin (the Sicilian coastal divisions, the Bastogne perimeter, the German south face at Falaise and a few others), they start stretched along the whole line.
  • Attack posture: each order carries a setting for how hard the attack presses: Probe, Deliberate (the default), Determined or Whatever the cost. It changes the odds the commander wants before he attacks, the cohesion at which he halts, and how long his battalions hold on before falling back. You can change it while the order runs.
  • Staff estimate: while you draw an objective line, the card shows the expected arrival time, the odds against the enemy you know of near the objective, the odds the commander wants, and roughly how many hours of fighting his supply will cover.
  • Halts: a commander who stops says why: the odds are too poor (and what he wants), or the odds are good but his battalions are too disorganised to attack, with the hours out of contact they need.
  • Fire discipline: formations ration fire as their stocks run down (Auto). Set Free to fire at full rate whatever the stock, or Conserve to make ammunition last at some cost in effect.
  • March fatigue: foot troops tire over about 16 hours on the move, faster at night and off roads, and recover over about 8 hours at rest. Tired, Exhausted and Spent troops move slower, fight less well and lose cohesion if driven on. Motor and tracked units tire far more slowly. The unit table shows each battalion's state.
  • Counter-battery: guns are not seen by their flashes from the front line. A firing battery is located over 30 to 90 minutes of fire by sound ranging, flash spotting and air observation (faster with a reconnaissance mission flying, faster for US and British gunners). Once located it can be engaged until it moves or stays quiet for an hour.
  • Cut off: an encircled battalion loses about 1 percent of its strength a day to sickness and wounds that cannot be evacuated, 4 percent once stocks run out; airborne and veteran troops lose nothing for two days. A battalion surrenders only when it has stayed shattered for two hours, is out of ammunition, is outnumbered three to one close by and has no friends within 5 km.
  • Your staff: formations handed to the staff may move on their own to an objective under attack and break out of a pocket (tick box in the forces panel), and may draw on the support pool if you allow it. Staff orders route round other columns' lines of advance, or wait for them to pass.
  • Reports: after each attack the formation reports its losses and the enemy's, the ground gained and what caused most of the damage. The end screen lists losses by cause and shows how your result compares with battles the computer fought on both sides.
  • The enemy's plan: an attacking computer side starts with the historical plan or an alternative that sends some formations against a neighbouring objective, and shifts its main effort when an attack stalls for twelve hours.
  • Cohesion ceiling: cohesion recovers only up to 40 percent plus 60 percent of morale, lower when troops are tired. The unit table shows both figures.
  • Reading the map: the selected formation shows its headquarters' reach, its guns' range and the road its supply columns use. The Forces list marks formations that are cut off, halted, refused, low on supply, crossing, waiting on a next leg or without orders. The reports can show all, alerts only, or the selected formation. Hover a card's Strength, Cohesion, Disruption, Morale or Supply for what it means. The end screen adds a timeline of who held each objective, and losses by day.
  • Orders: click a unit to select its formation. Draw a line of advance, adjust the objective line, then issue. The commander picks routes, deploys his battalions, uses cover, and calls artillery and air support himself.
  • Defend: draw only an objective line and the formation deploys along it and digs in.
  • Friction: orders issued before the clock starts are free. After that each order takes staff time, and changing an order under way costs cohesion. Lines of advance that cross each other delay and disrupt both formations, and disruption lowers combat power and speed until it wears off. A commander who judges the task impossible will halt or refuse and tell you why. You may relieve him.
  • Support tab: attach corps artillery, engineers, armour and special units to the selected formation. Engineers rebuild blown bridges.
  • Air tab: set each group to air cover, close support, interdiction or bombing, and mark zones and targets. Nothing flies at night or in fog, rain, snow or overcast.
  • Airborne: drops scatter with darkness, wind, low cloud and flak, lose transports to fighters and guns, and wait for flying weather. Select a waiting airborne formation in the Forces list to move its drop zone, delay the lift or hold it.
  • Flak: light flak (German 2cm and 3.7cm, Allied Bofors and M16 quad .50) covers about 2 km and is deadly to low-flying fighter-bombers, reconnaissance and transports. Heavy flak (German 8.8cm, US 90mm) covers about 5 km and hits medium and heavy bombers hardest. Every aircraft that crosses an umbrella risks being shot down, on the way in and out as well as over the target, and flak in the way widens airborne and supply-drop scatter. Night, cloud, losses and short ammunition weaken it. Each flak unit has a role button: AA covers the sky but fires weakly at ground targets beyond 800 m; AT turns its guns on tanks and leaves the sky open. The 8.8cm and 90mm are dangerous anti-tank guns. Flak cover a point (formation card) sends the formation's flak to dig in over a bridge, depot, airfield or drop zone. Flak that fires gives away its position to the enemy aircraft it fires at. Corps light flak sits in each side's Support pool.
  • Fog of war: you see an enemy unit only while your troops have it in sight or aircraft are over it. Nothing else on the map reveals it: enemy orders, depots, supply roads and columns stay hidden until spotted. Once out of sight a unit leaves a dashed marker with a ? where it was last seen, fading over three hours; it may have moved. Enemy depots appear once your troops come within 5 km or aircraft fly over them. Darkness, fog, rain, woods and towns shorten sight.
  • Air reconnaissance: in the Air tab, set a group to Recce and mark up to four areas (or let it fly over the enemy formations in turn). Each two-aircraft sortie reports the enemy units it sees within 7 km as solid markers with an R and the time of the report. Units under cover, dug in or standing still are harder to spot. Reports locate units to within a few hundred metres and age out after two hours. Recce flies only by day in flying weather, and flak can shoot it down.
  • Morale: every battalion has morale as well as cohesion. Cohesion is order under fire and comes back within hours; morale moves slowly. It falls with losses, long spells in contact, being cut off, short ammunition or rations, a lost attack, air attack, an HQ out of reach and neighbours who break. It rises with rest, a won fight, a captured objective, a skilled commander and supplies that get through. Elite troops (airborne, Rangers, commandos, Guards, SS, Fallschirmjaeger) start higher; coastal and scratch units lower. Morale caps how far cohesion recovers and sets how soon a battalion falls back. Below a quarter a battalion breaks: it runs for the rear, stops firing and shakes those near it, and rallies only once out of contact. A cut-off battalion whose morale collapses surrenders when the enemy closes. The unit list shows Steady, Shaken, Wavering or Broken.
  • How the battle is judged: the briefing carries guidance from the headquarters above you. It names what must be taken and by when, what must be held, what your government will and will not accept in losses, and any objective that carries weight beyond the map. The line at the top of the screen shows how that headquarters sees the battle so far. At the end it gives its assessment against what happened historically: a success that comes late or costs too much counts for less, and so does one that lets the enemy escape.
  • Cut off: supply columns travel only through ground your side holds. A formation with no such road from any depot is cut off: wounded cannot be evacuated, so it loses strength steadily, and once its stock runs low it loses order and morale as well.
  • Air supply: the formation card has an Air supply drop button. Click a point and transports fly in eight loads, in daylight and flying weather only, a few lifts a day. Enemy troops and flak near the drop point shoot down part of the load, and cloud and flak scatter the containers. The nearest troops in fighting order recover them, so containers that drift towards the enemy feed him. Drops that wait a day without flying weather are cancelled. The computer staff flies drops to its own cut-off formations too.
  • Supply: every formation draws ammunition, fuel and rations from its headquarters, and truck columns carry them up from depots. A depot on the map shows as a dump of crates and fuel drums that shrinks as its stock runs down; a supply road from off the map shows as a coloured arrow at the map edge. Your columns show as supply symbols with their load, and a dashed line marks the road still to drive. The Support tab lists depots, stocks and every column on the road; click a row to find it. Trucks keep to roads and drive across country only for the last stretch to a headquarters. Desert, towns and beaches count as drivable ground. Open fields count half, since farm tracks cross them. Past 3 km off the road each column carries less, down to half a load, and the card shows the distance. Supply landed by air is shared out on the spot. A battalion draws supply only if open ground joins it to its headquarters: ground away from the enemy's immediate reach (1.2 km round his troops), and no water without a bridge. A battalion cut off that way lives on what it carries and loses strength once its ammunition runs low. A lodgement across a river is fed by its boats while they last. Every battalion draws rations and water each day, half as much again in the desert. A depot overrun by the enemy hands him about a third of its stock as captured stores until they run out. Supply flown to airborne drop zones uses the same daily lift as the Air supply drop button.
  • Breaching minefields: on maps with minefields, a formation with engineers has Breach minefield on its card. Click the minefield: the engineers go to its near edge and clear a lane along the line from the formation, at about 200 m an hour. Enemy fire on the lane head stops the work. By day any enemy within 1.5 km stops it, and at night only an enemy within 800 m does, so night is the time to breach. Enemy guns with an observer halve the pace. A cleared lane drives like open ground and costs nothing in mine casualties, for either side. The engineers stay at the lane until told to rejoin.
  • River crossings: where a bridge is down or held, press Assault crossing on a formation's card and click the river. Its rifle battalions assemble on the near bank while trucks bring the assault boats up by road. Each boat carries a squad, so 26 boats lift about two companies a trip. Every trip runs under the fire of enemy troops near the far bank, and of their guns if they have an observer. Our troops and guns on the near bank, the men already across, darkness and fog all cut the losses. Each wave lands at once and fights, and a battalion fills out as its later waves arrive. Once no formed enemy has stood within 1.5 km of the landing for half an hour, the bridgehead is secured. A bridge set trucked to the site lets the engineers bridge the river, at the 1944 manual's rates, slower at night and stopped by fire on the site. Wider rivers need more than one set. Shellfire can break a floating bridge, and engineers mend it in two hours. The battalions across hold the bridgehead until told to rejoin, and Take up the bridge frees the set for use again.
  • Interdiction: a column plans its route around enemy-held ground. A column that finds the enemy on its road turns back with its load and the staff looks for another route; with no route left the formation is cut off. Enemy units that meet a column destroy it, enemy guns shell any column their units can see, aircraft on interdiction hit columns in their zones, and bombers can strike depots. An overrun depot is lost until you retake it. Do the same to the enemy: cut his roads, take his depots and put aircraft over his supply routes. Enemy columns stay hidden until your troops see them or aircraft on recce or interdiction spot them; aircraft on interdiction strike the columns they find in their zones.
  • On arrival: the formation card has an On arrival row: hold, dig in, rest and refit, exploit, delay or reserve, with an optional time limit. A commander with a high aggression rating may seize an opening on his own when told to hold, dig in or sit in reserve. His own moves show in amber.
  • Relief: the Relieve button names the replacement (a veteran, an experienced officer or the deputy) and the cost. Relief costs least out of the line, most during an attack, and each repeat within a day costs 35 percent more.
  • Markers: every order draws on the map: arrows for moves, brackets and lines for hold, dig in, delay, exploit, reserve and rest. A settled marker fades after two hours and returns at full strength when you select the formation or it comes under fire. The "show orders" box hides the rest.
  • Saves: the Save button stores the battle in this browser or as a file. The game autosaves every two game hours and when you leave the page. Loading replays the battle to the saved minute.
  • Bridges: when a bridge on a formation's route goes down, the formation stops and waits. It never sends its battalions round by another crossing. Engineers rebuild the bridge: a formation with its own engineers sends them up, and one without shows an Attach engineers button on its card. Rebuilding takes time and needs the bridge clear of enemy fire.
  • Fires: the Support tab lists corps and division artillery. Give each battery a mission. DS attaches it to the selected formation. R keeps it under corps and adds its guns to that formation's fights. GSR covers the whole front with first call to that formation. GS covers the whole front, takes the threats closest to your line and enemy batteries, or a priority target you mark. Batteries firing on one target mass their effect, most with US and British fire direction. A new mission takes 20 minutes to plan.
  • Command and doctrine: each headquarters reaches a set distance, longer with a skilled commander and good radios. Battalions beyond it recover cohesion at half rate and fire less well. Reconnaissance, commandos, detached units and corps guns are exempt. On the move out of contact a formation keeps to its slowest battalion's pace; reconnaissance and commandos range ahead. National doctrine sets staff time and initiative: German orders are fastest and junior commanders act most on their own, British set-piece planning is slower, Italian orders slowest with the shortest reach.
  • Alerts: N jumps to the latest alert and steps back through older ones. With "contact (slow to 1x)" ticked under "Stop the clock on" in the Forces panel, the clock drops to 1x when one of your formations meets the enemy at 12x or 40x.
  • Cancel and detach: Cancel order (or Backspace) withdraws an order; while the staff is still preparing it, this costs nothing. In the unit list, tick battalions and press Detach to send them to a point on the map. They dig in and hold it on their own, and ignore the formation's orders until you tick them and press Rejoin.
  • Held ground: every half-kilometre of the map belongs to one side, tinted blue for Allied and red for Axis, with the front line drawn where they meet. At the start the front line is the historical one for that date (for the tutorial, the Cane River), and assault landings start with all the land in enemy hands. Ground changes hands by these rules: a unit in fighting order takes the ground within 1.5 km of it (artillery 0.8 km) unless enemy troops in fighting order stand within 1.2 km; whoever takes an objective takes the ground within 1 km of it; and every hour, empty ground almost surrounded by the other side's ground goes over to that side, so bypassed pockets fill in. Shaken or retreating units, headquarters and ships take no ground. Your map shows the true picture within 4 km of your units, around objectives and where aircraft have looked; elsewhere it keeps the last picture your staff had. T or the Forces-tab checkbox toggles the tint.
  • Objectives: the strip under the top bar lists every objective with its owner colour. A percentage shows an objective being taken. Click one to jump to it.
  • Sound: M or the Sound button toggles sound. Gunfire, guns, explosions and alert chimes follow the camera and play at 1x and 4x speed.
  • Graphics: G cycles High, Medium and Low. F3 shows the frame rate. H opens this card. The game pauses when you leave the window.
  • Clock: Space pauses, 1-4 set speed. Watch a fight at 1x; use 12x or 40x for approach marches.

Orders of battle, commanders and equipment figures are compiled from general reference knowledge and simplified to battalion level. Treat unit lists and performance numbers as approximate until checked against a named source.

Lines of Advance

Operational command, 1943-44

You command a corps or an army in real time. For each formation you draw a line of advance and an objective line. Its commander chooses the route, deploys his battalions, digs in, and calls for guns and air support himself. You decide where, when and with what.

Supply, weather, terrain and fog of war shape every battle. Orders take staff time, plans meet friction, and an opponent that learns from its mistakes answers each move. Tell a formation what to do on arrival (hold, dig in, rest, exploit, delay or reserve) and it carries on without you.
